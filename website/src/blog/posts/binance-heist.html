I spend my days in Cortex XDR at an MSSP, watching for the moment an attacker turns a trusted tool against the people who own it. On 29 October 2026 that happened at a scale nobody had seen before. In about twenty minutes, Binance hot wallets on ten blockchains paid an attacker $6.36 billion. Four times Bybit. The largest theft on record, of any kind.

The strange part is what did not happen. No private key was stolen. No smart contract bug, no protocol flaw. Binance's own signing system produced every signature the attacker needed, on chains that do not even share a key type. This post reconstructs how that is possible from the on-chain record and the forensic reports, then turns it into hunts for Cortex XDR and the controls that would have stopped it.

The short version

On October 29, 2026, an attacker drained about $6.36 billion from Binance hot wallets on ten blockchains, most of it in roughly 20 minutes. No private key was stolen. The attacker made Binance's own signing system sign for them.

  • Who: TRM Labs (high confidence) and Chainalysis attribute it to TraderTraitor (UNC4899, Jade Sleet, Slow Pisces), the North Korean unit behind the $1.5B Bybit theft. Binance has not confirmed attribution.
  • Entry: Binance's first finding was a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop that reached a service container able to submit withdrawals. It later found "additional points of breach" it has not described.
  • The trick: on Ethereum, a batch of 50 pre-signed EIP-7702 authorizations turned a hot wallet into a sweeper the attacker controlled. On the other nine chains, wallets simply signed normal transfers to the attacker.
  • Lesson for defenders: the private key was never the weak point. The path from an engineer's endpoint to the system that asks the signer to sign was. That path is detectable on the endpoint, in the cloud control plane and at the signer's policy layer.

This post walks the attack chain, then gives Cortex XDR hunting queries for each stage and mitigations ranked by how directly they would have stopped it.

Two traits repeat. First, the target is always someone with production reach: Safe said its Developer1 was one of the very few staff with elevated access. Second, the attacker lives off the victim's own cloud and DevOps tooling, so nothing looks like classic malware after day one.

Teng said the breach was not a "one-click" action using a single engineer's permissions. That fits the 2025 pattern of chaining several identities and service accounts before reaching the money path.

One more data point matters. On October 27, two days before the theft, Binance said it had blocked several North Korea-linked attacks in the previous week, reportedly from the "Contagious Interview" fake-recruiter campaign. No source links that campaign to this breach, but it shows the exchange's engineers were being actively targeted.

DMM Bitcoin is the closest structural match. There, the attacker never touched the keys either: they sat inside the transaction-request workflow and altered a request that a legitimate employee had made. Binance's "service container that submits withdrawals" is the same choke point, one layer further into production.

From laptop to signer: why no key leaked

The attacker did not need Binance's keys, because they could get Binance's signer to sign anything. That is TRM's core finding, and Binance's own statement points the same way.

An exchange hot wallet is built to sign fast and without humans. The usual architecture has three layers:

  1. Requesters: internal services (withdrawal processors, rebalancers) running in containers that build transactions and submit them for signing.
  2. Policy layer: rules that decide whether a request is allowed (destination allowlists, amount limits, velocity caps, transaction types).
  3. Signer: an HSM or MPC cluster holding the key material, which signs whatever the policy layer approves.

Binance said the laptop gave access to "a service container" that could "submit unauthorized withdrawal transactions". That is the requester layer. From there, the attacker's requests had to pass the policy layer, and on ten chains they did.

The evidence that the signer itself was driven, rather than keys stolen:

  • Wallets on Bitcoin, Ethereum, Tron, XRP Ledger, Solana, Arbitrum, Optimism, Base, Avalanche and Polygon all produced valid signatures within about half an hour. These use different key types and signing schemes (secp256k1 on the EVM chains and Bitcoin, Ed25519 on Solana), so one stolen key could not explain it.
  • The 50 EIP-7702 authorizations were signed in advance, as a batch, across a range of nonces. With raw key access, the attacker would just sign one authorization at the right nonce at execution time. TRM reads the batch as a sign that access was time-limited, or that signatures had to be requested through a system.
  • Teng has said the 7702 delegation bypassed Binance's normal approval controls. So either the policy layer did not understand type 0x04 authorizations, or the attacker reached a path that skipped it.

One more on-chain fact settles who held the key afterwards: the revocation at 00:10:35 on October 30 was signed by Binance 14 itself. Binance still controlled that key, so the attacker borrowed signing capability rather than walking off with it.

Controls that did not fire

Binance has published nothing on its control environment, but the on-chain record shows six places where a control was missing or silent:

  1. Message-type policy. The signer produced type 0x04 delegation authorizations for a treasury wallet. An exchange hot wallet has no routine reason to sign one.
  2. Batch and nonce-range check. One request yielded 50 authorizations spanning nonces 18,914,775 to 18,914,824, all ahead of the wallet's live nonce.
  3. Cross-chain velocity. No aggregate limit tripped while 17 wallets on 10 chains paid one actor inside half an hour. Per-wallet limits cannot see that pattern.
  4. Delegation circuit breaker. Binance's top-up automation sent 200M USDT into Binance 14 at 21:42:59, 78 minutes after it had been emptied, and the attacker swept it 72 seconds later.
  5. Inherited-authority inventory. Binance 14 was the executor of a Circle smart account, so the hijacked address also unlocked 823M USDC.
  6. Revocation speed. The delegation stayed live for 3 hours 49 minutes. Tether, Circle, Ripple and the Arbitrum Security Council all acted before Binance cleared it.

For detection engineering, the takeaway is that the crown jewel is not the key store. It is every identity and workload that can talk to the signer's request API.

EIP-7702: turning a hot wallet into a sweeper

One valid authorization signature gave the attacker standing control of Binance's main Ethereum hot wallet until revoked. EIP-7702 shipped in the Pectra upgrade in May 2025 and adds transaction type 0x04 (set code), which carries an authorization_list.

Each entry is a tuple [chain_id, address, nonce, y_parity, r, s]. The wallet's key signs this digest:

Authorization digest

keccak256(0x05 || rlp([chain_id, address, nonce]))

When the transaction executes, the EVM writes a delegation designator into the wallet's code slot: 0xef0100 || address. From then on, any call to the wallet runs the delegate contract's code in the wallet's own context, with its balance and token approvals. Three properties made this the perfect drain primitive:

  • Anyone can submit it. The authorization is signed by the wallet, but the 0x04 transaction can be sent and paid for by any account. Here the operator submitted it.
  • It is nonce-bound. An authorization is valid only if its nonce equals the wallet's current account nonce. Binance 14's nonce climbs with every withdrawal, so the attacker pre-signed tuples for nonces 18,914,775 to 18,914,824 and packed all 50 into one type-4 transaction. In the same block, Binance's own withdrawal used nonce 18,914,808, so the tuple for 18,914,809 was the first valid one. Invalid tuples are skipped rather than reverting the transaction, and each applied tuple bumps the nonce, so the higher tuples simply re-applied the same delegation.
  • It persists. The delegation stays until the wallet signs a new one. The sweeper only obeyed the operator, so every deposit arriving after 20:24 was swept again, six more times by 23:56.

The hot wallet also acted as the executor of a separate Binance smart account, which let the attacker pull a further 823M USDC from it before Circle froze it.

A second Ethereum hot wallet sent 28,006 ETH, 55.7M USDT and 25.9M RLUSD by plain signed transfers with no delegation. 7702 was a convenience for the busiest wallet, not the core of the breach.

Detection hook: a delegated EOA has code. eth_getCode on a treasury address returning anything starting 0xef0100 means the wallet now runs someone else's logic. The same is true of any 0x04 transaction whose authorization list names a wallet you own.

web3.py: poll treasury addresses for a delegation

# Poll treasury EOAs for EIP-7702 delegation (web3.py)
from web3 import Web3
w3 = Web3(Web3.HTTPProvider(RPC_URL))
for addr in TREASURY_EOAS:
    code = w3.eth.get_code(addr)
    if code[:3] == bytes.fromhex("ef0100"):
        delegate = "0x" + code[3:23].hex()
        alert(f"{addr} delegated to {delegate}")

The sweeper

Sweeper v2 is 1,674 bytes of Solidity 0.8.28 with unverified source; the interface below is a third-party reconstruction from bytecode, so function names are guesses. The sweep selector is 0x8a179be4.

Sweeper v2, reconstructed interface

// Reconstructed behaviour of sweeper v2 (names inferred)
function sweep(address to, address[] calldata tokens) external onlyOperator;
//   sends the full balance of each listed token, plus ETH, to `to`
function withdrawETH(address to, uint256 amount) external onlyOperator;
function withdrawToken(address token, address to, uint256 amount) external onlyOperator;
function callExecute(address target, address to, uint256 value, bytes calldata data)
    external payable onlyOperator;
//   makes the delegated wallet perform any call, e.g. execute() on a smart account it owns
// onlyOperator: msg.sender must equal the hard-coded 0x7C86...5CA0, else revert

The hard-coded caller is the design choice that matters. Once Binance 14 ran this code, nobody else could call it, so Binance could not front-run the sweeps and had to sign a fresh authorization to evict it. callExecute is how the attacker drove the Circle smart account that Binance 14 owned and pulled the 823M USDC.

Laundering and the freeze race

Assets with an issuer were caught and assets without one were not: about $2.84B is frozen (Elliptic, verified on-chain), while the attacker still controls roughly $2.4B to $3.3B of ETH, BTC, XRP and SOL depending on the snapshot. The stablecoins sat in attacker wallets for 37 minutes on Ethereum and 46 on Tron before the first freezes, and the attacker used that window to sell at any price.

Route What happened
Intent-based fills \~325M USDT sold to market makers through UniswapX and solver contracts for \~122,300 ETH, an implied \~$2,650 per ETH against a \~$2,300 market
Panic swap 100M USDT swapped in one transaction with zero slippage protection for 34,583 ETH, about 22% below value
Lending as an exit 59.9M USDT supplied to Aave v3 and 19,762 WETH borrowed between 20:58 and 21:01; frozen collateral, unfreezable ETH
Tron 300M USDT into JustLend; 234M USDT swapped for 638M TRX on SunSwap; thousands of fresh addresses each seeded with exactly 1 TRX
Token dumps UNI, PEPE, ENA and \~150 smaller tokens sold for \~70,480 ETH, some into pools far too thin to absorb them
Cross-chain Only \~$1.9M so far: TRX through a \~28-hop peel chain, THORChain and the Hyperlane bridge to the hub shared with Bitget proceeds
Bitcoin, XRP, SOL Untouched since the first hour

Two tooling details stand out. The swaps ran through MetaMask's stock EIP-7702 delegator and the Uniswap Universal Router, so the attacker used 7702 for laundering as well as theft. Chainalysis describes the redistribution as scripted and unlike anything it had seen before, and Bitquery estimates about $119M lost to slippage.

Freezes so far: Tether about 1.77B USDT, Circle about 825M USDC, Ripple 40.5M RLUSD, World Liberty Financial 27.8M USD1, and the Arbitrum Security Council about $30M in ETH and ARB by forcing transfers through governance.

What this does to attribution

TRM (high confidence) and Chainalysis name TraderTraitor; Elliptic says "likely" but urges caution. The laundering is the main reason for doubt:

  • For: Tornado-funded staging, rehearsal on test wallets and pre-obtained signatures fired in one burst, all matching Bybit; the fake-recruiter attempts on Binance staff the week before.
  • Against: DPRK operators normally dump stablecoins within minutes, yet $2.6B sat long enough to be frozen. Market makers and lending markets replaced the bridge-heavy Bybit pattern. The Bitget overlap rests on about 13.63 ETH (\~$39,000) of Binance funds at an address that may be a shared laundering service.
  • Missing: no FBI, OFAC or South Korean statement. After Bybit, the FBI named North Korea within five days.

Detections with Cortex XDR

No single rule catches this campaign; six hunts across four layers give overlapping chances. Each query below is XQL you can run as a hunt or save as a correlation rule. Field names follow the standard xdr_data and cloud_audit_logs schemas, so confirm them against your tenant before scheduling.

# Layer What it catches Stage
1 Endpoint DevOps tool binaries running from odd paths (fake kubectl) Execution
2 Endpoint Unsigned children of IDEs, Docker or Python reaching the internet C2
3 Endpoint Non-CLI processes reading cloud and cluster credentials Credential access
4 Cloud One identity's session used from several IPs, or an offensive user agent Session hijack
5 Kubernetes Humans exec-ing into, patching or reading secrets of wallet workloads Lateral movement
6 Signer 7702 authorizations, nonce-range batches, multi-chain signing bursts Impact

1. Masquerading DevOps binaries

The 2025 UNC4899 case dropped a backdoor named like the Kubernetes CLI. Real tooling lives in a handful of install paths; anything else with that name is suspect.

Cortex XDR (XQL)

dataset = xdr_data
| filter event_type = ENUM.PROCESS and event_sub_type = ENUM.PROCESS_START
| filter agent_os_type in (ENUM.AGENT_OS_MAC, ENUM.AGENT_OS_LINUX)
| filter action_process_image_name in ("kubectl", "docker", "aws", "gcloud", "terraform", "helm")
| filter not (action_process_image_path ~= "^(/usr/local/bin|/opt/homebrew/bin|/usr/bin|/Applications|/snap/bin)/")
| fields _time, agent_hostname, actor_effective_username, action_process_image_path,
         action_process_image_command_line, actor_process_image_name, action_process_image_sha256

2. Lure-spawned implants calling out

The lures run inside a developer's trusted tools: an IDE task, docker compose up, npm install or a Python script. Flag unsigned processes in that causality chain making external connections.

Cortex XDR (XQL)

dataset = xdr_data
| filter event_type = ENUM.NETWORK and agent_os_type = ENUM.AGENT_OS_MAC
| filter causality_actor_process_image_name in ("Code Helper (Plugin)", "Cursor Helper (Plugin)",
         "python3", "node", "docker", "com.docker.backend", "Terminal", "iTerm2")
| filter actor_process_signature_status != ENUM.SIGNED
| filter action_external_hostname != null
| comp count() as conns, values(action_external_hostname) as hosts by agent_hostname,
       actor_process_image_path, actor_process_image_sha256

Enrich hosts with domain age: Bybit's C2 was two days old. Any lookup under 30 days on an engineer laptop deserves triage.

3. Cloud and cluster credential theft

After landing, the attacker reads whatever the engineer is already logged into. Watch reads of credential stores by processes that are not the official CLIs.

Cortex XDR (XQL)

dataset = xdr_data
| filter event_type = ENUM.FILE and event_sub_type = ENUM.FILE_OPEN
| filter action_file_path ~= "/\.(aws/(credentials|sso/cache/.+)|kube/config|config/gcloud/.+|docker/config\.json|azure/.+)$"
| filter actor_process_image_name not in ("aws", "kubectl", "gcloud", "docker", "terraform",
         "helm", "k9s", "Code Helper (Plugin)", "Lens")
| fields _time, agent_hostname, actor_process_image_path, actor_process_signature_status, action_file_path

4. Hijacked sessions in the cloud control plane

Bybit's attacker reused live AWS session tokens, timed to the developer's working hours, from ExpressVPN exits with a Kali user agent. A stolen session shows up as one identity spread across new IPs.

Cortex XDR (XQL)

config timeframe = 24h
| dataset = cloud_audit_logs
| filter identity_type in ("AssumedRole", "FederatedUser", "user")
| comp count_distinct(caller_ip) as ip_count, values(caller_ip) as ips,
       values(user_agent) as agents by identity_name, cloud_provider
| filter ip_count > 2 or arraystring(agents, ",") ~= "(?i)kali|distrib#"

Pair this with the endpoint: an engineer's session token used from an IP that is not their laptop's egress IP is a strong signal. Also alert on new MFA device registration and MFA policy edits, both tried in the 2025 cases.

5. Kubernetes moves toward wallet workloads

The 2025 case edited deployments for persistence, leaked a CI/CD service account token into logs, and pulled database creds from pod env vars. Binance's "service container" sits at the same layer.

Cortex XDR (XQL)

dataset = cloud_audit_logs
| filter operation_name ~= "(?i)pods[./](exec|attach|portforward)|deployments[./](patch|update)|secrets[./](get|list)|serviceaccounts[./]token"
| filter resource_name ~= "(?i)wallet|signer|withdraw|custody|treasury|hsm|mpc"
| filter not (identity_name ~= "^system:")
| fields _time, identity_name, caller_ip, operation_name, resource_name, user_agent

Also flag new privileged pods, hostPID or hostPath mounts, and container image changes in those namespaces outside a CI pipeline run.

6. The signer: where impact becomes visible

This is the layer that would have caught Binance's breach regardless of entry point. It needs the signing service's audit log shipped to Cortex as a custom dataset; here it is signer_audit_raw, with fields you will map from your own HSM or MPC logs.

Cortex XDR (XQL)

// 6a: any EIP-7702 authorization request on a treasury wallet
dataset = signer_audit_raw
| filter tx_type = "0x04" or authorization_count > 0
| alter nonce_span = max_auth_nonce - min_auth_nonce
| fields _time, requester_identity, requester_workload, wallet_id, authorization_count,
         nonce_span, delegate_address

// 6b: one requester signing across many chains or wallets in minutes
dataset = signer_audit_raw
| bin _time span = 5m
| comp count() as sigs, count_distinct(chain) as chains, count_distinct(wallet_id) as wallets,
       sum(amount_usd) as usd by _time, requester_identity
| filter chains >= 3 or wallets >= 5 or usd > 10000000

// 6c: destination not on the withdrawal allowlist
dataset = signer_audit_raw
| join type = left (dataset = withdrawal_allowlist | fields address as allowed)
       as al al.allowed = destination_address
| filter allowed = null and wallet_tier = "hot"

TRM recommends treating a request for many authorizations across a range of nonces as an indicator of compromise. Rule 6a should page someone instantly; there is no benign reason for a treasury wallet to sign 50 delegations at once.

Finally, ingest the on-chain poller from the EIP-7702 section through an HTTP collector, so a 0xef0100 code prefix on any treasury address lands as an alert next to the endpoint and cloud signals.

Before you rely on these: the hunts are written from public reporting, not from Binance's telemetry, and none of them ran on a live exchange. Dataset and field names depend on how your tenant ingests endpoint, cloud and Kubernetes data. The signer queries assume you ship your signing service's audit log as a custom dataset with fields you map yourself. Run each query once on your own data before you schedule it.

Mitigations

The key store held; the request path and the signer's judgment failed. The controls below are ranked by how directly they would have cut this attack, and they apply to any exchange, custodian or fintech running automated signing.

1. Give the signer its own judgment

HSMs and MPC protect the key, not the decision to use it. The policy engine in front of the signer must reason about what it is signing. For hot EOAs, the allowed set should be plain value transfers and token transfer() calls; everything else (EIP-7702 authorizations, approve, permit, setApprovalForAll, upgrades, arbitrary calldata) is denied unless a separate quorum approves it.

A minimal policy, written in Rego for OPA:

Signer policy (Rego for OPA)

package signer.policy
import rego.v1

default allow := false

deny contains "EIP-7702 authorization on a hot wallet" if {
    input.tx.type == "0x04"
    input.wallet.tier == "hot"
}

deny contains "more than one authorization in a request" if {
    count(input.tx.authorization_list) > 1
}

deny contains "chain-agnostic authorization (chain_id 0)" if {
    some a in input.tx.authorization_list
    a.chain_id == 0
}

deny contains "authorization for a non-current nonce" if {
    some a in input.tx.authorization_list
    a.nonce != input.wallet.current_nonce
}

deny contains "destination outside registry with no withdrawal record" if {
    not input.tx.to in data.registry.addresses
    not input.request.withdrawal_id
}

allow if {
    count(deny) == 0
    input.request.intent_verified
}

Any one of the first four rules would have refused the 50-authorization batch. The chain_id 0 rule matters too: a tuple with chain ID 0 is valid on every EVM chain, so one signature could be replayed against the same address on any 7702-enabled chain where its nonce matches.

2. Verify intent, not just the requester

DMM Bitcoin and Binance share one lesson: an authenticated service submitting a request proves nothing about whether the request is legitimate. Fix it with two independent trust domains:

  • The withdrawal ledger signs an intent (customer ID, asset, amount, destination, request ID) with its own key, run by a different team in a different cloud account.
  • The signer, or an independent policy co-signer inside the MPC quorum, rebuilds the transaction from that intent and refuses if a single byte differs.
  • Internal moves (rebalancing, top-ups) may only go to a registry of exchange-owned addresses that changes through a separate, multi-person process.
  • Requesters authenticate with workload identity (mTLS with SPIFFE-style certificates bound to the pod), so a session lifted from a laptop cannot impersonate the container.

3. Shrink the blast radius

  • Run separate signer clusters and policy engines per chain family and per wallet tier. No single orchestrator should be able to request signatures on ten chains.
  • Enforce velocity caps per wallet, per chain and in aggregate across all chains per 5, 15 and 60 minutes. A global breach halts all hot signing, not just the wallet that tripped.
  • Size hot wallets to hours of outflow. Binance 14 alone held 167,464 ETH and 1.6B USDT.
  • Inventory every right a hot EOA holds elsewhere: smart-account ownership, executor roles, token allowances, admin keys. Move them off hot wallets. Delegated code inherits all of them, which is how 823M USDC left a Circle account.

4. Make eviction fast

A revocation is itself an EIP-7702 authorization to the zero address, and it is nonce-bound like any other, so it cannot be usefully pre-signed. What you can prepare is the path to sign it live:

  • A break-glass signing route for the revocation, outside the normal request service, tested quarterly. Binance needed 3 hours 49 minutes.
  • A per-block eth_getCode monitor on every hot EOA. A 0xef0100 prefix triggers an automatic halt of all top-ups and deposit consolidation into that wallet, then pages the on-call. This alone would have saved the 200M USDT top-up swept at 21:44.
  • The same halt on any outflow pattern the wallet has never produced before.

5. Close the engineer-to-production path

  • Anyone who can reach wallet infrastructure works from a privileged access workstation, not their daily laptop. Block AirDrop, Bluetooth file transfer and unmanaged USB on those machines.
  • No standing production access. Use just-in-time, time-boxed, approved sessions through a recorded bastion with FIDO2 MFA.
  • Bind sessions to the device and keep cloud tokens short-lived, so stolen cookies and tokens fail when replayed elsewhere (the core of both DMM and Bybit).
  • Run every coding test, "help me debug" project and unknown repo in a disposable cloud sandbox with no credentials. Brief engineers on fake-recruiter lures; Binance staff were being targeted that very week.
  • On macOS, enforce an EDR policy that blocks unsigned binaries launched from IDE workspaces, Downloads and Docker volumes, and deploy the Cortex hunts above.

6. Lock down wallet namespaces

  • Put wallet services in their own cluster and cloud account. Deny kubectl exec, attach and port-forward in those namespaces through RBAC and an admission policy, with a logged break-glass role.
  • Apply Pod Security Admission at restricted: no privileged pods, no hostPID, no hostPath.
  • Pull secrets from a vault as short-lived dynamic credentials, never from environment variables.
  • Admit only signed images (cosign or equivalent), and ship deployment changes only through CI with two-person review.
  • Use NetworkPolicy so that only the request service can reach the signer, and alert on any interactive shell in a wallet pod.

7. Have the halt-and-freeze runbook ready

  1. Halt all hot signing and deposit consolidation with one global switch. Binance's suspension came over an hour after Tether's first freeze.
  2. Evict any delegation with a zero-address authorization and confirm eth_getCode returns empty.
  3. Freeze by sending collector addresses to pre-agreed 24/7 contacts at Tether, Circle, Ripple, Paxos and L2 security councils. Tether's 37-minute response still let \~$786M convert to ETH and TRX.
  4. Share addresses with TRM, Chainalysis, Elliptic and SEAL 911 so exchanges and solvers screen at the fill.
  5. Preserve the laptop image, container filesystem and signer logs before cleanup. Safe{Wallet}'s attacker wiped malware and bash history.
  6. Rotate every credential reachable from the compromised laptop and container, and assume they are all burned.

Still unknown

  • How was the DevOps laptop compromised: fake recruiter, trojanized project, supply-chain package, or something new? Is it linked to the "Contagious Interview" attempts Binance reported blocking on October 27?
  • What are the "additional points of breach", and do they include identities beyond the first engineer?
  • How did the service container reach signing requests on ten chains: a policy bypass, a misconfigured rule, or a direct path to the signers?
  • Why did 50 type 0x04 authorizations pass: was the message type simply unknown to the policy engine?
  • Does the address 0x4885...7c8e, which received withdrawals from Binance 14 and 16 in September and later paid the Bitget hub, point to an attacker-controlled Binance account?
  • Will a government attribute the theft? Until the FBI, OFAC or South Korea does, exposure to these funds is stolen-property risk; after, it becomes sanctions risk.
  • How long was the attacker inside before October 28? In the Bybit case, 17 days passed between the developer's infection and the theft.
